What the nomination says

About 250 acres of historic houses

The National Register nomination for the Sanford Residential Historic District, listed on December 15, 1989, describes about 250 acres in central Sanford. It runs roughly from South Elm Avenue to South Palmetto Avenue and from Thirteenth Street up to West Second and Third Streets. The period of significance is 1880 to 1933.

The nomination says most houses are wood frame with weatherboard or drop siding, and that brick piers and brick foundations are common. Most of the district's buildings date from the 1920s boom, mainly bungalows and apartments between Seventh and Thirteenth Streets. The earliest houses stand around Magnolia and Palmetto Avenues.

Practical meaning

Where the plumbing questions come from

A wood-frame house on piers can leave open space under the floor. If yours does, a plumber can look at supply and drain lines there instead of opening walls. Tell us about that access when you call.

The nomination also says many original metal and wood roofs were replaced with asphalt, composition or crimped metal. That is a fact about roofing, and we do not draw plumbing conclusions from it. What it does mean is that owners here are used to dealing with restoration rules.

Historic district rules

Check with the city before exterior work

The City of Sanford says a Certificate of Appropriateness is required before any exterior work, including repairs, in its Residential Historic District. Its Historic Preservation Board meets on the third Wednesday of each month.

The city page does not describe interior plumbing. If your plumbing job could change the outside of the house, ask the city first, and ask your plumber to schedule that part after you hear back.

General information

Services people in older neighborhoods ask about

  • Hidden leaks: Leak detection avoids cutting into finished walls and floors until the source is found.
  • Old supply lines: If leaks keep returning, repiping is the question to ask about. We do not assume your pipes need it.
  • Water heater age: A plumber can tell you whether replacement makes more sense than another repair.
  • Before you buy: A sewer camera inspection shows the condition of the main drain.

We have no record of the plumbing in any individual house here, so the list above is general. See the Sanford plumbing page for how calls work across the city.

Practical checklist

Questions to ask before any plumber starts

  • Where is the main shutoff? Show it to the plumber and make sure everyone in the house knows where it is.
  • Is there access under or behind the work? Say whether the house has a crawl space, an attic hatch or an access panel.
  • Is a permit needed? Plumbing work in Florida falls under the Florida Building Code, Plumbing. Ask the City of Sanford building division what your job needs.
  • Will finished surfaces be disturbed? Ask in advance about patching and painting, and who handles it.

Sources for the local facts above: National Park Service nomination for the Sanford Residential Historic District, Wikipedia entry for the district listing date, and the City of Sanford historic preservation page (sanfordfl.gov).
